How each page is made

Lunar occultations

The 25 events for 2026–2029 were picked by hand from a DE431 scan of every time the Moon covers a planet or bright star. For each, the engine maps where on Earth it is seen and traces the graze limits. For each city it finds the contact times, then corrects them for the Moon's uneven edge. That moves them by a few seconds at most. Each page carries a 2 KB description of the event, from which your browser solves any place, light time and aberration included.

When the Moon rises or sets during an occultation, a place sees only half of it; the page says which half.

The Moon and the stars

Each month, September 2026 to December 2028, is one file: the Moon and Sun as daily curves, and the stars the Moon can cover. Your browser finds every occultation for your location. Each instrument has a rule of thumb for how faint a star can be at the Moon's dark and bright edges. Moonglare and twilight lower it, and the Moon must be 5° up. Each page prints the rule.

Asteroid occultations

Each month, September 2026 to August 2027, the site's own integrator moves all 4,511 asteroid orbits forward and tests every Gaia star in reach. It includes the planets and Moon from DE431, Ceres, Pallas, Vesta and Hygiea, and the Sun's relativistic term. For each shadow, the engine draws the centre line, both edges and the 1σ lines.

The 1σ combines the star's error with the orbit's. The orbit error is the largest of three: JPL's formal error, the gap between JPL's and the Minor Planet Center's orbits ÷ √2, and 10 mas. Independent orbits differ by about 10 mas, far more than JPL's formal error.

A path is listed if it passes within 100 km of an Indian city at night, with the star 10° up. It must be at least as wide as its error, with a fade you can notice. 947 so far.

Each event has a finder chart (Gaia stars to G 14 within 15′, with the asteroid's hourly track), your chord, a fade sketch, a world map, and the path as KML or GPX. It opens on its own page, with the path on a street map. Drop a pin to see what any spot gets, then share it. Or let the page suggest five stations across the path; spread-out timings reveal the asteroid's shape. The full track around the world is drawn too, bright where the star is up after dark. Press Play to watch the shadow cross. As elsewhere, your browser works out how close the path comes to you, when, and for how long.

Jupiter's and Saturn's moons

Eclipses, occultations, transits and shadow transits of Jupiter's four big moons and seven of Saturn's come from JPL's satellite ephemerides. The planet is a flattened globe casting a cone of shadow. That gives 3,882 events for Jupiter and 5,302 for Saturn, 2026–2028. Each one happens at the same instant everywhere. The page only checks whether the planet is up and the sky dark where you are.

The Great Red Spot

A storm, not a body with an orbit. The site computes when the spot's longitude reaches the middle of Jupiter's disc (System II), using DE431 and the IAU pole. The spot drifts, so its longitude is carried forward only to 31 December 2026. After that, no transits are shown until it is updated.

Calendar feeds

Each of 516 cities gets a feed with the lunar occultations it can see, binocular star occultations for twelve months, and asteroid shadows passing over or near it.

What is approximate

  • The Moon's edge. City tables and the solid graze lines use the real lunar terrain. Times for “your location”, the shaded map and the world map treat the Moon as a smooth sphere, good to about 2 seconds.
  • The horizon. Altitudes are geometric, for sea level, without atmospheric refraction or hills and buildings. Near the horizon the Moon looks about half a degree higher than shown. Your skyline decides the rest.
  • Earth's rotation. ΔT for future dates is an estimate, good to well under a few seconds through 2029.
  • Grazes. Near a graze limit a few hundred metres north or south changes what you see. The graze profile on each event page shows how sensitive your spot is.
  • Seeing a star. Visibility is a rule of thumb. Haze, light pollution, your instrument and your eyes decide. The aperture presets on the asteroid pages are estimates too. Each is about a magnitude brighter than what the aperture can detect, since a star at the limit is too faint to see fade. A camera reaches 2–3 magnitudes deeper.
  • Jupiter's and Saturn's moons. The brightness drop in a mutual event assumes evenly lit discs. Saturn's rings are not modelled, so “behind Saturn” means behind the globe. The moon diagrams are illustrations, good to a tenth of the planet's radius. Shadows and eclipses are drawn from the listed times, which come from JPL.
  • The Great Red Spot. Its times are only as good as its assumed longitude: each degree off moves them by 1.65 minutes.
  • An asteroid's shape. Every path, time and duration is worked out for a sphere of the asteroid's catalogue diameter. Real asteroids are lumpy, so the shadow may be narrower or wider. Timings from several places measure this. Where DAMIT has a reliable model (rated 2 or better of 5, with a settled pole), the event page draws the asteroid's real shape with your chord across it: 98 of 947 events. These shapes are convex and scaled to the catalogue size. Their rotation is projected from light curves, often over decades. They are shown, not used.
  • An asteroid path's position. Errors of a few milliarcseconds shift a path sideways by kilometres to tens of kilometres. The red dashed 1σ lines show how much. Each place gets a percentage chance, assuming the real path may be off by a normal 1σ error, which is how occultation software reads a prediction. The chance is only as good as the 1σ. Occult and OccultWatcher use their own orbits, so their numbers may differ. Near an edge, expect either outcome. A star Gaia flags as hard to fit (high RUWE) is often double, and may fade in steps or not at all.
  • The list. The lunar occultations are a hand-picked selection, not every one that happens. The asteroid list covers asteroids 15 km and up and stars to G 12.5, on paths near Indian cities at night at least as wide as their error.
